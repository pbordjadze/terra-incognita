# An ocean remembered in a meal — evidence record

Independently authored historical content for an offline atlas experiment. Checked by a research agent, 26 October 2026 (ET). This is a documented source check, not a human specialist's review.

## Compact prose available for the encounter

**A seed:** A grain can outlast the meal it belonged to. Excavations at early settlements in Madagascar and the Comoros recovered Asian rice. At several of these sites, rice dominated the surviving food crops. It is evidence of daily life, not a passenger list.

**An echo:** Malagasy belongs to the South East Barito branch of the Austronesian language family, whose other members are spoken in southern Borneo. A nearby coast is not always a language's closest relation. Malagasy and Ma’anyan share inherited vocabulary; Malagasy also carries words borrowed through contact with other languages.

**A word to hold:** Malagasy **zato**; Ma’anyan **jatuh**. Both mean **hundred**. They are related forms, not a recorded conversation across the sea. A second, different kind of connection: Malagasy **sambo**, “ship”, and Old Malay **sambaw**, “ship”, belong to the history of borrowing and maritime contact. Do not present Malay as Malagasy's parent language.

**The islands between:** On the Comoros, early sites also held rice, mung bean, and cotton. Crowther and colleagues interpret this as evidence that the Southeast Asian presence extended beyond Madagascar. The finds do not establish one exact voyage or a direct route. Comorian languages today are Bantu languages.

**People, not an arrow:** The lives behind these traces are harder to recover than the traces themselves. The early migrants' names and individual motives are not given by a seed or a word. Malagasy histories also include deep African connections: Bantu-language contact helped shape Malagasy, and genetic studies find both African and Asian ancestry across the sampled communities. Neither a language nor ancestry percentages define a person's identity.

## Claim ledger

### L1. Linguistic relationship: Madagascar and South Borneo

- **Claim:** Malagasy is a South East Barito language; this subgroup's other languages are predominantly spoken near the eastern banks of the Barito River in South Borneo.
- **Place/time:** Modern language relationship used to reconstruct historical connections; not a dated map of ancient exclusive territories.
- **Source:** Alexander Adelaar, “Who Were the First Malagasy, and What Did They Speak?”, in *Spirits and Ships: Cultural Transfers in Early Monsoon Asia* (ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, 2017), pp. 441–469. The publicly accessible introduction states the relationship and region. [Publisher introduction](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/spirits-and-ships/who-were-the-first-malagasy-and-what-did-they-speak/FB1D39086A9D294A71E2CE42E018F01D).
- **Exact short passage:** “It is a member of the South East Barito ... language subgroup”.
- **Scope:** The introduction also discusses Malay, Javanese, Sanskrit via Malay/Javanese, Ngaju, and South Sulawesi contacts. This is a layered history; the Borneo connection is not an exclusive ancestry or complete cultural explanation. The chapter's full paid text was not inspected; its publisher-provided introduction was.
- **Status:** Checked against the scholar's introduction, corroborated by L2's full paper.

### L2. The two printed word examples and African language contact

- **Claim:** Malagasy **zato** and Ma’anyan **jatuh** mean “hundred”; Malagasy **sambo** and Old Malay **sambaw** mean “ship”. The former is an inherited comparison; the latter belongs to contact/borrowing history.
- **Source:** Alexander Adelaar, “A linguist’s perspective on the settlement history of Madagascar”, *NUSA* 61 (2016), pp. 69–88. [Institutional full PDF](https://tufs.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1402/files/04Adelaar.pdf), [persistent record](http://hdl.handle.net/10108/89605).
- **Locators:** Table 1 on printed p. 72 (`jatuh`, `zàtu`, hundred); §8 on printed p. 84 (`sàmbu`, Old Malay `sambaw`, ship). Spelling conventions on printed p. 70 explain his use of `u` for official Malagasy `o`, final `i` for official `y`, and added stress marks. The encounter restores standard Malagasy spellings, **zato** and **sambo**, without stress marks.
- **Exact short passage for interpretive limit, p. 76:** “The identity, role and status of the ancestors of the early Malagasy migrants before they left South Borneo remains uncertain.”
- **Scope:** Full PDF inspected. §5 and §7 discuss Bantu contact and disputed migration routes; different disciplines produce competing models. Do not present Adelaar's favored indirect route or seventh/eighth-century dates as settled facts. Do not animate a morph from one modern word to another as if one directly became the other. No speech recording or synthetic pronunciation is cleared here.
- **Status:** Checked full paper and printed table. Standard spellings and meanings were additionally checked in the *Malagasy Dictionary and Encyclopedia*: [zato](https://malagasyword.org/bins/teny2/zato), [sambo](https://malagasyword.org/bins/teny2/sambo), and [vary](https://malagasyword.org/bins/teny2/vary). These dictionary entries confirm forms and glosses; they are not being used to establish a migration route or the debated etymology of vary.

### A1. Early crop evidence around the western Indian Ocean

- **Claim:** Archaeobotanical assemblages from tested Comorian and Malagasy sites dated to the eighth–tenth centuries CE were dominated by Asian crops, with rice especially abundant. Tested coastal East African assemblages were instead predominantly African crops; this is a sampled archaeological comparison, not a timeless divide.
- **Source:** Alison Crowther and colleagues, “Ancient crops provide first archaeological signature of the westward Austronesian expansion”, *PNAS* 113.24 (2016), pp. 6635–6640. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1522714113), [author-institution full PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2270857_9/component/file_2300820/content), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4914162/).
- **Locator:** Published Table 1, Fig. 1, and Results and Discussion subsection on African versus Asian crop evidence (PDF pp. 2–3; published pp. 6636–6637). Sima has rice, mung bean and cotton; Mahilaka has rice and cotton. Do not say mung bean was recovered at Mahilaka or all sampled Malagasy sites.
- **Exact short passage:** “the earliest archaeobotanical assemblages date from the 8th to 10th centuries CE and are dominated by Asian crops”.
- **Limitations:** Burnt and preserved remains do not measure all meals or all crops. Cotton species cannot be distinguished from seed morphology alone; the authors infer the Asian species. Rice and mung bean have South Asian domestication histories, so do not say they were domesticated in Borneo. The authors interpret the combined crop pattern with linguistic and genetic evidence as support for Southeast Asian settlers; a grain alone does not identify an individual's language or ethnicity.
- **Comoros:** The “Were the Comoros Part...” discussion (PDF p. 5, published p. 6639) treats earlier Comorian sites as possible evidence of Austronesian settlement there before Madagascar; preservation and sampling biases remain possible. No exact route is established.
- **Status:** Full PDF inspected. The app should label c. 700–1000 CE as the broad period of these assemblages, not the first settlement date. A broader 700–1200 CE window may describe the overall study but should not be labeled the Borneo-to-Madagascar migration.

### H1. Human context without genetic essentialism

- **Claim:** A large island-wide genetic study found African and Asian ancestry components in all the sampled Malagasy individuals, with substantial regional and individual variation. This corroborates layered population histories; it does not define contemporary cultural identity or recover individual past motives.
- **Source:** Denis Pierron and colleagues, “Genomic landscape of human diversity across Madagascar”, *PNAS* 114.32 (2017), E6498–E6506. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1704906114), [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28716916/), [full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5559028/).
- **Locator:** Abstract (2,704 individuals from 257 villages), Results, “Origin of Malagasy Genomic Diversity”; Discussion and Fig. 6. The study authors favor heterogeneous admixture events in Madagascar, rather than a single already-mixed founding group; this differs from some linguistic models and must remain attributed.
- **Exact short passage:** “the admixture was sex biased and happened heterogeneously across Madagascar”.
- **Scope:** Abstract and indexed full-text passages inspected. No ethnicity heat map or single percentage should be used. Prefer the sober qualitative claim in the encounter.
- **Status:** Checked. This research record does not claim access to the underlying individual genetic data.

## Spatial and temporal semantics

- Madagascar and southern Borneo may be linked by a **relationship line** only, labeled explicitly as a relationship rather than a voyage route. Do not animate a ship traveling that line.
- Map pins for Sima (Anjouan, Comoros) and Mahilaka (northwestern Madagascar) represent archaeological sites. Use approximate coordinates unless surveyed coordinates are obtained; label them approximate.
- The rice drawing is an original editorial illustration, not a rendering of a particular excavated specimen. No field photograph is licensed by this record.
- A time reveal changes **which archaeological traces are being considered**, not the coastline, boundaries, or population distribution.
- A seed-to-language transition is an editorial juxtaposition of independent evidence. It must not claim that rice caused the linguistic relationship.
- The movable reveal aperture is an editorial way to inspect traces, not a simulation of digging or a reconstruction of a buried landscape. Any seeds or hatching revealed through it are original, nonquantified illustrations. Their number, spacing and density do not encode recovered specimen counts, crop proportions, settlement intensity, or the extent of a historical population. The caption and evidence notes supply the archaeological scope.

## What remains uncertain

The dates, stages and routes of migration; the social status and motivations of early migrants; how many voyages or waves occurred; how representative the surviving archaeobotanical samples are; and how the historical patterns map onto any person's lived identity. No named early voyager, speech, vessel, exact crossing or personal anecdote is supplied by these sources.
