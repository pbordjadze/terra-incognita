# The Wave: sources

What is real, what is drawn, and where each comes from. No data file is bundled: the stadium, the
crowd and the city are worked out from the constants and rules in `src/`.

## The phenomenon and its model (real)

- I. Farkas, D. Helbing & T. Vicsek, "Mexican waves in an excitable medium", *Nature* 419 (2002)
  131–132 (the preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0210073, read for this toy). From 14 waves in
  stadiums of over 50,000 people: "a typical velocity in the range of 12m/s (20 seats/s), a width of
  about 6-12m (~15 seats) and more frequently rolls in the clockwise direction. It is generated by
  the simultaneous standing up of not more than a few dozens of people". Their model: people are
  excitable units, activated when "the weighted concentration of active people within a radius of R
  around a person is above the threshold of the person ci (randomly chosen from [c-∆c, c+∆c])";
  weights fall exponentially with distance and lean one way; after activation a person goes through
  na active and nr refractory states. "Triggering a Mexican wave requires a critical mass."
- That rule is `crowd.rs`, with a seat a cell and a step 1/28 s. The paper's figure gives na = nr = 5,
  c = 0.25, ∆c = 0.05, R = 3 in its own units; here na = nr = 20, c = 0.42, ∆c = 0.05, R = 3 seats,
  a fall-off of 1.5 seats and those behind counting twice those ahead, chosen so that the model's
  wave runs 20 seats a second, about 14 seats wide, and groups of 16 rarely start one while groups of
  30 usually do (the tests in `crowd.rs` measure it).
- The paper's model, left alone, runs a wave round for ever, and every wave started keeps going, so
  a few swipes leave the bowl striped with waves. Real waves end. So three things are added here, not
  from the paper and not measured, chosen so that a wave lasts a lap or two (one and a half to three
  minutes) and the crowd will start another about half a minute after one dies: each time a person
  stands their threshold rises by about 0.1, falling back over about 45 s (so a second wave close
  behind the first meets people who have only just sat down, and fizzles); each second everyone's
  threshold wobbles by up to 0.06 and the whole crowd's drifts by about 0.015 (some crowds are keener
  than others); and while a wave runs the whole crowd's threshold rises 0.001 a second, falling back
  over 15 s once none is running. As it rises the least keen only half stand, so the wave dims before
  it breaks. A group of a few people somewhere tries on its own every 10 to 30 s while no wave runs;
  about one in seventeen tries is a bigger group, and a wave starts on its own about once in seven
  minutes (`crowd.rs`' measurements: `cargo test -p wave --release -- --ignored --nocapture`).
- The wave was "first widely broadcasted during the 1986 World Cup held in Mexico" (the same paper).

## The place (real, simplified)

- **Estadio Azteca**, Mexico City, which held the 1986 final; about 87,500 seats today (it held well
  over 100,000 in 1986). Here 87,000 seats in 58 rows round one rounded-rectangle rake; the real bowl's
  three tiers, roof and exact plan are not modelled.

## The look and the sound (drawn)

- Night, the floodlights down, every person holding up a phone's light (most, not all); standing, the
  light rises and brightens. The pitch, the stadium's shell and the city's streets round it (a grid of
  orange street lights, avenues every few blocks) are drawn in `bowl.wgsl` and `city.wgsl`.
- The crowd's murmur and the roar that follows the standing people round the bowl are synthesised in
  `sound.rs`; no recordings.
