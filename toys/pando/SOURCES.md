# Pando: sources

What is measured, what is drawn, and from where. No data file is bundled: the grove, the stem, the leaf
and its skin are drawn from code (src/), and they move by the rules below.

## The place (measured)

- **Pando**, a clone of quaking aspen (*Populus tremuloides*) in Fishlake National Forest, Utah, about a
  mile southwest of Fish Lake on State Highway 25 (38.525° N, 111.75° W, the toy's `PLACE`). It covers
  about 43 hectares (106 acres) with more than 40,000 stems (the Forest Service; EarthDate gives more than
  47,000), and is one genetic individual on one root system (J. DeWoody, C. A. Rowe, V. D. Hipkins and
  K. E. Mock, "'Pando' lives: molecular genetic evidence of a giant aspen clone in central Utah", *Western
  North American Naturalist* 68 (2008) 493–497; USDA Forest Service, *Pando*; EarthDate, *Pando: A Forest
  of One*). The coordinates are Wikipedia's.
- **The autumn gold**: as the chlorophyll fades the leaves' carotenoids and xanthophylls are unmasked
  (National Park Service, Rocky Mountain National Park, *Aspen*). The stems of one clone tend to turn
  together (USDA Forest Service, *Aspen fall colors*), though how strictly is debated; the toy shows only
  Pando, all of it gold.

## The leaves (measured rules)

- **Why they quake**: the stalk (petiole) is flattened along its whole length, at right angles to the blade
  (USDA Forest Service, *Celebrating Wildflowers: Quaking Aspen*), so the blade twists about it in the
  lightest air. Each leaf here is a twisting spring with little damping, kicked by the wind's turbulence
  with a force growing as the wind's square.
- **How fast**: aspen leaves flutter at about three to five times a second (D. A. Roden and R. W. Pearcy,
  "Effect of leaf flutter on the light environment of poplars", *Oecologia* 93 (1993) 201–207; J. S.
  Roden, *Trees* 17 (2003) 117–126, modelled at 3 Hz). Here each leaf's own twist is 3–5 Hz.
- **The bark and its eyes**: green-white bark, coloured by chlorophyll in it (USDA Forest Service, above);
  black eye-shaped scars where the stem shed its lower branches (University of Montana, *This is Montana:
  Aspen*).

## The skin (measured rules, hurried)

- **The pores**: aspen leaves have their stomata on the underside only, spread evenly over it (R. Rushin
  and J. E. Anderson, *Plant Physiology* 67 (1981) 1264), which is the side the toy shows.
- **Open in light, shut in shade**: stomata open in light and close in darkness, the guard cells holding
  chloroplasts. In a real leaf this takes minutes to tens of minutes (in *Arabidopsis*, opening with a time
  constant of about 36 minutes: Flütsch et al., *The Plant Cell* 32 (2020) 2325); here it is hurried to a
  few seconds (opening 3 s, closing 1.4 s), as a time-lapse would.

## The wind (measured rules, drawn numbers)

- **Gusts over a canopy**: the waves that run over a windy crop or canopy ("honami"; S. Dupont et al.,
  *Journal of Fluid Mechanics* 652 (2010) 5–44). The eddies that make them are carried along at about 1.8
  times the wind at the canopy's top (after M. R. Raupach, J. J. Finnigan and Y. Brunet, *Boundary-Layer
  Meteorology* 78 (1996) 351–382), and the gusts here travel at that speed.
- **How a stem sways**: trees of this size sway about once every two to four seconds (Norway maples of
  8.5–10 m at 0.29–0.56 Hz: *Dendrobiology* 64, 37–42; a modelled sycamore at 0.26 Hz: T. Jackson et
  al., 2019); each stem here sways at 0.25–0.45 Hz.

## Drawn (not measured)

- Every number the sources don't give: the breeze (1.6 m/s), the gusts' strength, size and how often they
  come, how a finger's drag becomes a gust, how far a crown leans, how many leaves show their undersides
  for a wind, the leaves' and branches' layout, the cells' size and how many pores there are, the
  vapour leaving the open pores, and the light.
- **The look**: late September at evening, the ridges of the Fishlake Plateau, the stems three metres
  apart, and the gold, all made up after the place.
- **The sound**: the leaves' hiss and patter and one leaf's flutter, drawn from noise.
