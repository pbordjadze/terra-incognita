A note in the margin

A continent, written in water.

Run a finger across the blue lines. A touch follows a river to its mapped ending. Switch to Gather, and the tributaries that lead to your touch appear together.

Try a broad stroke, or stay with a single line. There is no route to finish.

What the ink means

The connections come from RiverATLAS v1.0, a river network derived from elevation data. A touch snaps visibly to the nearest displayed channel. The ink follows that dataset’s downstream links; Gather reverses those links. Colors distinguish connected river systems, not countries.

Only larger channels are shown: reaches with at least 500 km² of upstream land, plus their downstream connections. Gather shows the tributaries retained here, not every stream or a surveyed catchment boundary.

The flowing ink is an illustration of connectivity. Its speed is invented. This is not a simulation of rainfall, travel time, present flow, flood risk, or navigability. Distances are rounded sums of mapped channel lengths, not straight lines. The first partial reach is estimated from the simplified line.

The limits of a line

A branching dataset gives each reach one downstream connection. Real rivers can divide, shift, flood, flow through wetlands, and be changed by people. The Casiquiare, for example, links the Orinoco and Amazon systems; this simplified tree cannot express that connection. An inland ending does not mean an empty landscape.

These rivers are lived places, with many names and meanings. The few city and river labels here are orientation aids. A drainage network cannot stand in for the lives, histories, or knowledge of the people along it.

Sources you can inspect

Network and topology
Linke, S. et al. (2019), “Global hydro-environmental sub-basin and river reach characteristics at high spatial resolution.” Scientific Data 6, 283. RiverATLAS v1.0, South America. Article · Data. CC BY 4.0. Modified: larger reaches selected, chains merged, geometry simplified and rounded.

How connections were derived
Lehner, B. & Grill, G. (2013), “Global river hydrography and network routing: baseline data and new approaches to study the world’s large river systems.” Hydrological Processes 27, 2171–2186. The NEXT_DOWN field records the next downstream reach; zero means a coast or inland sink. Paper.

The Casiquiare exception
Laraque, A. et al. (2019), “Water and Sediment Budget of Casiquiare Channel Linking Orinoco and Amazon Catchments, Venezuela.” Water 11, 2068. Paper. An important limit of the single-outlet model.

Land, relief & names
Natural Earth, public domain, for coastlines and cities. GEBCO 2008, via NASA Earth Observatory and the bundled globe, for generalized relief. Relief is an original color rendering, not survey contours. River names identify nearby mapped channels; labels and coastlines are generalized. Equirectangular map, standard parallel 20° S.
